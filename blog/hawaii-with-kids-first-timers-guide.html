Hawaii With Kids: A First-Timer's Planning Guide

What to know before taking children to Hawaii: when to go, what to book early, sunscreen rules, and how to pace the days.

Photo: Kellen Riggin / Unsplash

Hawaii is a domestic trip for U.S. residents, which makes it one of the easier long-distance family vacations. There is no passport to worry about and no currency to exchange. The main challenges are the flight, the time change and fitting too much into too few days.

When to go

Hawaii is a year-round destination. Many families prefer the shoulder months, roughly spring and fall, when crowds and prices tend to ease compared with summer and the winter holidays. Winter brings bigger surf on north-facing shores and is also the season for humpback whale watching. Summer has calmer water on many north shores. Our Hawaii guide has a month-by-month view.

What to book early

  • Flights and rooms for school holidays and summer. These fill first.
  • Rental car: on islands other than Oahu, a car is nearly essential. Availability can be tight at busy times.
  • Popular activities such as Haleakala sunrise on Maui, which requires an advance reservation, and Pearl Harbor on Oahu, where timed entry is the norm.
  • Luaus and boat tours, especially for larger groups.

Pack for the sun and the sea

Hawaii has restricted the sale of sunscreens containing certain chemicals to help protect coral reefs, so bring a mineral-based sunscreen or buy one there. Add rash guards for kids, water shoes for rocky entries, and a light rain jacket, since mountain and windward areas get showers any time of year.

Pace the trip

Flying from the mainland costs you most of the first day, and the time difference can wake young children very early for the first few days. Build in slow mornings. A good rhythm is one main activity a day with beach or pool time around it.

Safety at the beach

Ocean conditions in Hawaii can change quickly, and some beaches have strong currents or shore breaks that are dangerous even for strong swimmers. Swim at beaches with lifeguards when you can, check posted warning signs, and never turn your back on the water. Ask the front desk or a lifeguard which beaches are best for your kids that day.

Be a respectful visitor

Stay out of closed areas, do not touch sea turtles or monk seals, and keep a safe distance from wildlife. Hawaii's communities and natural areas welcome visitors who treat them with care.
